You Already Use the Cloud Every Day & Where Your Photos Really Are

Slide 6/53 Slide 7/53
Everyday Examples (Slide 6/53):
  • Opened email in a web browser, not stored on your own disk.
  • Took a photo on phone ➔ appeared on laptop a minute later.
  • Watched a video without downloading the file first.
  • Bought a new phone, signed in, and messages came back.
  • Two people edited the same document at the same time.
Starting Question: Your phone holds a few hundred GB. Your email, photos, and videos are much larger. Where is all of it actually stored?
Where Your Photos Really Are (Slide 7/53):
  • They are files on hard disks, inside computers, inside a large building.
  • The building belongs to a company such as Google, Amazon, Microsoft, or Apple.
  • It may be in another city, or another country.
  • Your phone holds a copy, or only a small preview. Your phone is a window onto the files, not the place they live.
💡 What "Cloud" Really Means: "The cloud" is not something floating and invisible. It is other people's computers, in buildings, with electricity bills and security guards. You are renting space and time on them.

Two Ways to Get Computing Power: Own vs. Rent

Slide 8/53

🏢 Own the computers

  • You buy them and pay the full price at the start
  • You find a room, power, and cooling for them
  • You install, update, and repair them
  • They are yours whether you use them or not

☁️ Rent the computers (Cloud Computing)

  • Someone else buys and keeps them
  • You use them over the internet
  • You pay only for the hours you use
  • You can stop at any time

A Comparison You Already Understand: Electricity

Slide 9/53
⚡ How Electricity Works (= Cloud Model):
  • You plug in a device and the power is simply there;
  • You pay for the units you actually use;
  • You can plug in one lamp or twenty, and the supply still works;
  • Somebody else builds and repairs the power stations.
🛑 Where the Comparison Stops:

Electricity is all the same. Computing is not: your data has to be kept private, kept correct, and kept in a country whose laws you can live with. That is why this is a whole course and not one lecture.

4. Virtualization
  • Server — a computer that does work for other computers. Physically it is ordinary hardware mounted in a rack.
  • Data center — a building made to hold servers, with backup power, cooling, fire protection, and fast network connections.
  • Virtualization — software that splits one physical server into several separate virtual machines (VMs), each running its own operating system.
  • Hypervisor — the software that creates those virtual machines and shares the real CPU, memory, and disk among them.

📌 Cloud computing is built on virtualization: one physical computer can act as many separate ones.

A first definition: Using computers that someone else owns and operates, reaching them over a network, paying for what you use.

PART 2

What Makes Something Cloud Computing (NIST SP 800-145)

Slides 13/53 – 21/53

The Old Way: Everything in Your Own Building ("On-Premises")

Slide 13/53

"On-premises" means the organization owns and runs everything itself:

  • the building, the power, the cooling, the locks on the door;
  • the servers, the storage, the network equipment, the cables;
  • the operating systems and their updates;
  • and the staff who fix all of it at 3 a.m.
The most important consequence: you must buy the computers before you know how many people will use the system, and buying and delivery take weeks or months.

Guessing How Many Computers to Buy & The 40x Registration Spike

Slide 14/53 Slide 15/53

📈 Mistake 1: Buy too many

  • Enough for the busiest day of the year
  • On every other day, most of them do nothing
  • The money is already spent, and the machines lose value each year

📉 Mistake 2: Buy too few

  • Enough for a normal day
  • On the busiest day the system slows down or stops
  • It fails exactly when the most people need it
The Trap (Slide 14/53): When the number of computers is fixed, there is no right answer — only a choice of which mistake to make. Cloud computing removes the choice, because the number is not fixed.
🎓 Discussion Question & Answer (Slide 15/53):

Question: A university runs its student registration system on its own servers. During registration week, about 40 times as many students use it as in a normal week. How many servers should the university buy — and what do those servers do during the other 51 weeks?

✅ Answer: Pay for what you actually use, not for the busiest week you might have. (On-premises, 39/40ths of the servers would sit idle for 51 weeks).

The Standard Definition (NIST SP 800-145) & The 5 Essential Characteristics

Slide 16/53 Slide 17/53
NIST Special Publication 800-145 (Mell & Grance, 2011)
"Cloud computing is a model for enabling ubiquitous, convenient, on-demand network access to a shared pool of configurable computing resources (e.g. networks, servers, storage, applications, and services) that can be rapidly provisioned and released with minimal management effort or service provider interaction."

In plainer words: you can get computing whenever you want it, over the network, from a shared supply, and give it back when you are done, without asking anyone.

5 Essential Characteristics
4 Deployment Models
3 Service Models
Characteristic (NIST SP 800-145) What it means in practice
1. On-demand self-service You create what you need yourself, in seconds, without anyone at the provider approving it.
2. Broad network access You reach it over the network in a standard way, from a phone, a tablet, a laptop, or a workstation.
3. Resource pooling The provider's computers serve many customers at the same time, and you usually do not know which physical machine you are on.
4. Rapid elasticity You can add and remove capacity quickly, often automatically, and there always seems to be more available.
5. Measured service Everything you use is counted and reported — which is what makes paying by the hour possible.

Sharing One Computer Among Customers: Multi-Tenancy

Slide 18/53
Multi-tenancy: many customers use the same physical computer at the same time, kept apart by software.
✅ Why providers do it
A shared machine stays busy, so each customer pays less. This is why cloud is affordable.
⚠️ What it costs you
Another customer's heavy work can slow yours down, and your separation depends on the software being correct.
⚖️ When it is not allowed
Some laws and contracts forbid sharing a machine. This is one reason private clouds exist.

Growing and Shrinking: Scalability vs. Elasticity & Scaling Up vs. Scaling Out

Scalability

The system can be made bigger when more people use it. Usually a planned change, made by a person.

Elasticity

The system grows and shrinks by itself as the number of users changes. Shrinking is where the savings come from.

Is It Really Cloud? A Quick Test

Slide 20/53

If even one of the five characteristics is missing, it is usually not cloud computing.

• A server you get by emailing the IT office and waiting two days ❌ no on-demand self-service
• A rented machine fixed at 4 CPUs and 8 GB for a whole year ❌ no rapid elasticity
• A shared cluster where nobody counts who used what ❌ no measured service
⚠️ Exam Trap: Moving a program onto a virtual machine in your own data center is virtualization. By itself, that is not cloud computing.

What You Gain, What You Give Up, and CapEx vs. OpEx

Slide 21/53

✅ You gain

  • No large purchase at the start
  • Machines ready in minutes
  • Capacity that follows demand
  • The provider does the repairs

⚠️ You give up, or must watch

  • A bill that never stops, and grows if nobody checks it
  • Some control and visibility
  • Easy exit: leaving a provider is hard (vendor lock-in)
  • Choice of country: laws such as KVKK and GDPR decide where data may be kept
💰 Two Words from Finance: CapEx vs. OpEx

• CapEx: one large purchase of something you own, like servers.
• OpEx: a regular payment for what you use, like rent.
Cloud turns the first into the second. For a system that runs unchanged for many years, owning can still be cheaper.

Cloud Deployment Models

Slides 22/53 – 25/53

Two Separate Questions About Any Cloud System

Slide 22/53
1. Deployment model

Who owns the computers, and who may use them?

Public, private, community, hybrid, multi-cloud
2. Service model

How much of the system do you run yourself?

IaaS, PaaS, SaaS, FaaS
⚠️ Exam Rule: The two answers are independent: any service model can run on any deployment model. When a question asks for one of them, do not answer with the other.

The 5 Deployment Models

Slide 23/53
Model Who uses it Typical reason to choose it
Public Anyone who pays; the provider owns everything. Start fast, no purchase, growth without limit.
Private One organization only. Laws or contracts forbid sharing; full control.
Community A group of organizations with the same rules. Hospitals, universities, or agencies sharing costs.
Hybrid A private and a public cloud, connected. Sensitive data stays inside; extra machines come from outside when needed (cloud bursting).
Multi-cloud Two or more public providers side by side. Avoid depending on one provider; use each one's best service.

💰 Cost Warning: Hybrid and multi-cloud have a price: two sets of accounts, networks, and bills to run, and a charge each time data moves between them.

Public or Private?

Slide 24/53

🌐 Public cloud

  • Machines shared with other customers
  • Pay as you go; running in minutes
  • Best when demand is unknown or changes a lot

🔒 Private cloud

  • Machines for one organization
  • Large purchase up front; full control over everything
  • Best when rules forbid sharing, or demand barely changes
⚠️ A common misunderstanding: "Public" means anyone may buy the service. It does not mean your data is visible to others — as long as you set up the separation correctly.

Worked Exercise: Choose a Deployment Model for Each Case

Slide 25/53
1. Hospitals storing patient records under KVKK, with steady use. 1 ➔ Private
2. A small startup whose game may have 10 users or 500,000 next month. 2 ➔ Public
3. A bank keeping its accounts in-house but its website outside. 3 ➔ Hybrid
4. Five universities sharing one research system under the same rules. 4 ➔ Community
5. A company using one provider for video and another for its data. 5 ➔ Multi-cloud

The reason matters more than the label.

PART 4

Cloud Service Models & Shared Responsibility

Slides 26/53 – 30/53

The Three Service Models: IaaS, PaaS, SaaS

Slide 26/53
IaaS (Infrastructure as a Service)

You rent virtual machines, disks, and networks, and install everything on them yourself, including the operating system.

Examples: AWS EC2, Azure Virtual Machines, Google Compute Engine
🍳 Analogy: Renting an empty kitchen
PaaS (Platform as a Service)

You upload your program and the provider runs it. You never manage the machine or its operating system.

Examples: Azure App Service, Google App Engine, AWS Elastic Beanstalk
🥘 Analogy: Kitchen with everything prepared
SaaS (Software as a Service)

You use the provider's finished program, usually in a browser.

Examples: Microsoft 365, Google Workspace, Dropbox
🍽️ Analogy: Ordering the meal

Who Is Responsible for What (Shared Responsibility Matrix)

Slide 27/53 Slide 28/53
Layer Own building IaaS PaaS SaaS
1. Data and access You You You You
2. Application You You You Provider
3. Supporting software (runtime, web server, DB engine) You You Provider Provider
4. Operating system You You Provider Provider
5. Virtualization You Provider Provider Provider
6. Servers and storage You Provider Provider Provider
7. Building and network You Provider Provider Provider
Top Row Rule (Slide 27/53): The top row never changes: your data, and who can reach it, is always your responsibility.
Shared Responsibility (Slide 28/53): The provider protects the cloud itself; the customer protects what it puts in the cloud. Almost every large cloud data leak came from a customer's setting (storage left open, permission too wide).

Other "as a Service" Names

Slide 29/53
  • FaaS (Function as a Service), also called serverless — you upload one small piece of code; the provider runs it each time something triggers it and charges per run. There is still a server; you just never see or manage it. Examples: AWS Lambda, Azure Functions.
  • CaaS — Containers as a Service (Part 10).
  • DBaaS — a database run for you by the provider.
  • XaaS — "anything as a service", the general name.

Self-Check: Old Windows Program

Slide 30/53

Question: A team must run an old Windows program that needs one specific driver and one exact Windows version. Which service model fits?

✅ Answer: IaaS
  • PaaS lets you choose neither the driver nor the Windows version.
  • SaaS is someone else's program; there is nothing to install into.
  • IaaS gives you the whole machine — so its updates and backups are yours too.

  • Region — an area of the world where a provider has several data centers. Chosen for speed to users, price, and the country your data must stay in.
  • Availability zone — one data center (or a few) inside a region, with its own power and network, so one can fail without stopping the others.
📌 Design Rule: A system in one zone fails when that data center fails. To keep it running, place it in at least two zones — and pay for both.

How the Bill Is Calculated

Slide 33/53
  • You pay for the hours machines run, the data you store, and the data you send out.
  • Data coming in is usually free. Data going out usually is not; this is called egress. It makes leaving a provider expensive.
  • A stopped machine still costs money for its disk.
  • Free accounts are limited in time, in amount, or both.
🛡️ Before you use any provider: Set a spending alert at a low amount, and delete everything as soon as you finish an exercise.
PART 6

Troubleshooting Method

Slides 34/53 – 35/53

The Standard Six-Step Troubleshooting Method

Slide 34/53
1. Identify
Collect info, ask users, find out what changed.
2. Probable cause
Decide probable cause — start with the obvious.
3. Test that idea
If wrong, form a new one or ask for help.
4. Plan & repair
Plan the repair and carry it out (one change at a time).
5. Check
Check that everything works, and prevent a repeat.
6. Write down
Write down what you found and what you did.
❌ Two most common mistakes: Changing several things at once (so you never learn which one fixed it) and skipping step 6.
☁️ Cloud First Check: First check the provider's status page: it tells you in seconds whether the problem is theirs or yours.

Worked Example: Unreachable Website on a Cloud VM

Slide 35/53
Symptom: A website on a cloud virtual machine cannot be reached this morning. Yesterday it worked. Other systems in the same account are fine.
  1. Identify: Who cannot reach it? Since when? What changed last night?
  2. Probable cause: Other systems work, so the provider is fine. Something changed on this machine — a firewall rule, its address, or the program.
  3. Test: Is the machine running? Is the port open from outside? Is the program still running inside?
  4. Repair: One change at a time.
  5. Check: Test from outside, and add an automatic alert.
  6. Write it down: What changed, and what fixed it.
📌 Firewall rule: a setting that says which ports can be reached, and from where.

A Virtual Machine Is Made of Files

Slide 38/53

The hypervisor gives each virtual machine:

⚡ vCPU — a share of the real processor
🧠 memory — a slice of the host's RAM
💾 virtual disk — usually one large file on the host
🔌 virtual network card — connected to a virtual switch inside the host
Why this is useful: A whole machine is a few files plus settings. So it can be copied in a minute, moved to another host, and saved before a risky change so you can go back.

Modern processors have built-in hardware support (Intel VT-x, AMD-V), so a virtual machine runs almost as fast as the real one.

PART 8

Processors and Memory in Virtual Machines

Slides 39/53 – 40/53

Sharing the Processor (vCPU & Overcommitment)

Slide 39/53
  • A physical processor has several cores; each core runs one stream of work.
  • A vCPU is not a core you own. It is a turn on a real core, given many times a second by the hypervisor.
  • Overcommitment: giving the machines on a host more vCPUs in total than the host has cores.
✅ Usually fine
Most machines are idle most of the time, so they rarely need their turns at once.
🐢 Sometimes slow
When many get busy together, each waits for a free core. From inside, the machine looks healthy but is slow.

Memory, and Choosing the Right Size

Slide 40/53

⚠️ Memory is riskier to share than the processor. Processor time can be given in turns; memory cannot. When a host runs out, it moves memory to disk, which is hundreds of times slower.

📏 Right-sizing — choose the size of a machine from measurements, not guesses:
  1. Measure processor and memory use over a normal week;
  2. Choose a size for normal use;
  3. Handle busy times by adding machines, not by one huge machine.

Hypervisors also offer a reservation (a guaranteed minimum) and a limit (a maximum) for each machine.

PART 9

Virtual Machines in the Cloud

Slides 41/53 – 44/53

Creating a Machine in the Cloud (4 Choices)

Slide 41/53

Instead of installing from scratch, you make four choices, and the machine runs in about a minute:

1. Image — a saved copy of a disk: OS plus software (from provider, vendor, or your own).
2. Size — how much processor and memory. Families: general purpose, more processor, more memory, or GPU.
3. Network and firewall rules — which network it joins, and who may reach it.
4. Storage and a login key — its disk, and how you sign in.

Disks, and Stop vs. Delete

Slide 42/53
  • Local disk — inside the host. Fast, but erased when the machine stops. Only for temporary files.
  • Network disk — kept by the provider. Survives stopping, and can be moved to another machine. The normal choice.
  • Snapshot — a copy of a disk at one moment. Used for backups and for making new images.
⚠️ Stop Is Not Delete:
• Stopped: switched off. You stop paying for processor and memory, but still pay for the disk.
• Deleted: gone. Local disks go with it; a network disk may or may not, depending on a setting you chose.

Paying for Machines: On Demand, Reserved, and Spot

Slide 43/53
Way of paying What it means Use it for
On demand Full price per hour. No commitment. Unpredictable or short work.
Reserved Promise one to three years; pay much less. Work that runs all year.
Spot Spare machines, very cheap, but the provider can take them back with little warning. Work that can stop and restart, such as night-time batch jobs.

💡 Real systems mix them: reserved for the steady part, on demand for busy periods, spot for background work.

Autoscaling (Elasticity in Practice)

Slide 44/53
⚖️ Load Balancer
Spreads incoming requests & skips machines that stop responding
📈 Autoscaling Group (Min / Max)
Rule: CPU > 70% ➔ Add VM
CPU falls ➔ Remove VM

PART 10

Alternatives to Virtual Machines (Containers, Kubernetes, Serverless, Bare Metal)

Slides 45/53 – 48/53

Containers vs. Virtual Machines

Slide 45/53 Slide 46/53

Every virtual machine carries a full operating system. Ten machines running ten small programs means ten copies of the OS.
A container packs a program with the libraries it needs, but shares the operating system's core (the kernel) of the machine it runs on. Docker is the common tool; a container image is built once, stored in a registry, and runs the same way everywhere.

Feature Virtual machine Container
Contains Full operating system and program Program and its libraries
Size Gigabytes Megabytes
Starts in Tens of seconds or more Under a second
Separation Strong: its own kernel Thinner: shared kernel
Best for Old software, special drivers Many small services, updated often

📌 They are not rivals: in the cloud, containers usually run on virtual machines.

Other Forms You Will Meet

Slide 47/53
  • Kubernetes — software that runs many containers across many machines: it places them, restarts failed ones, and adds more when needed. Every provider offers it as a service.
  • Serverless functions — from Part 4: only your code, no machine or container to manage.
  • Bare metal — a whole physical server rented to you, with no hypervisor and no other customers. For strict rules or licenses.
  • Virtual desktops — your desktop runs in the data center; your device only shows the screen.

Choosing the Right Form

Slide 48/53
If the work needs... Use
A specific OS version or driver Virtual machine
Many small services, updated often Containers (with Kubernetes)
Short tasks triggered at unpredictable times Serverless functions
No sharing of hardware at all Bare metal
The same controlled desktop on any device Virtual desktops

Do not ask "which one is newest". Ask "what does this work require?"

8-Point Lecture Summary

Slide 51/53
  1. Cloud computing: using computers someone else owns, over a network, paying for what you use.
  2. It has five essential characteristics; if one is missing, it is usually not cloud.
  3. Deployment models: who owns it and who may use it.
  4. Service models: how much you run yourself.
  5. Your data, and who can reach it, is always yours to protect.
  6. Virtualization lets one physical computer act as many.
  7. A cloud machine is an image, a size, storage, and network rules.
  8. Containers share the operating system; choose the form the work requires.
