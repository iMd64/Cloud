PART 1

Starting Simple: What a Connection Needs

Slides 3/42 – 5/42

Where We Are & Core Networking Rules

Slide 3/42

Recap from the Cloud Networking Topic:

  • A cloud network has an address block, cut into subnets.
  • Route tables decide where traffic goes; the most specific route wins.
  • Firewall rules decide what is allowed; DNS turns names into addresses.
  • Address blocks must not overlap if networks are to be joined.
Core Technical Question: When a database runs in the cloud, how do you connect an office network and a second cloud network to it privately, and how do you systematically find what broke when the office cannot connect?

What a Connection Needs (The 5 Mandatory Checks)

Slide 5/42
STEP 1
1. Name

The name gives the right address, as seen from the client.

STEP 2
2. Route there

The client side has a route to the server's range.

STEP 3
3. Route back

The server side has a route to the client's range.

STEP 4
4. Firewalls

Every filter on the path allows the traffic.

STEP 5
5. Program

A program listens on the port and accepts the request.

Request: Client (10.0.8.10:51000) ➔ Server (10.20.4.15:5432)
Reply: Server (10.20.4.15:5432) ➔ Client ephemeral port (10.0.8.10:51000)
  • The request goes to 10.20.4.15:5432. The reply comes back to the client's temporary port (an ephemeral port, for example 51000). Each direction is routed and filtered separately.
  • All five must hold at once; one failure breaks the connection.
  • Every connection type today supplies only some of them. Troubleshooting checks them in this order (1 ➔ 5).
PART 2

Connecting an Office to the Cloud: VPN & Dedicated Links

Slides 6/42 – 13/42

Why Not Just Use the Internet?

Slide 6/42

The office could reach the database over the internet, only if the database had a public address and an open port.

❌ What Goes Wrong (Public Internet)

  • The database is exposed on the internet; source limits and TLS reduce the risk but do not remove it.
  • Traffic crosses the internet readable unless every program encrypts it.
  • Every new system needs another public address and rule.

✅ What We Want Instead

  • The office and the cloud network behave like one private network.
  • Private addresses on both sides.
  • Traffic protected on the way.
Two Ways to Achieve This: (1) A VPN over the internet, or (2) A dedicated connection.

VPN: A Private Tunnel Through the Internet

Slide 7/42

A VPN (virtual private network) creates an encrypted tunnel across the internet. Traffic inside it cannot be read or changed unnoticed on the way; with routes and rules in place, each side reaches the other's private addresses.

Site-to-site VPN

Joins two whole networks, such as the office and the cloud network. It runs all the time between two devices; users notice nothing.

Remote-access VPN

Joins one person's laptop to a network, usually started by the user.

  • Most site-to-site VPNs use IPsec, a standard set of protocols for encrypting and checking IP traffic.
  • Both ends must agree on its settings, or the tunnel does not come up.
  • Comparison: An armored truck on a public road. The road is shared; what is inside the truck is not.

  • Encapsulation: The VPN device wraps each packet inside a new one. The internet forwards it using the outer header only.
  • The cloud gateway removes the wrapper and routes the inner packet by its private destination.
  • Encryption covers only the part between the two VPN devices.
  • The wrapper also adds bytes, which matters in Part 8 (packet size / MTU).

What a Site-to-Site VPN Needs (6 Checklist Items)

Slide 10/42
1. VPN gateway on the cloud side: Created by you, run by the provider.
2. VPN device in the office: A router or firewall, usually with a fixed public address.
3. Compatible settings on both ends: The same pre-shared key or trusted certificates, and the same encryption options.
4. Routes on both sides: For the other side's address block.
5. Firewall rules on both sides: That allow the needed traffic.
6. Address blocks: That do not overlap.
⚠️ Exam Trap: "A tunnel can be up while the application is down"

Items 1–3 bring the tunnel up. Items 4–6, together with DNS and a listening program, decide whether the application works. Some providers (AWS, for example) also build each VPN connection as two tunnels; configure both.

Dedicated Private Connections

Slide 11/42

A dedicated connection is a physical link from your network to the provider, joined at a meeting point (a colocation data center) where both have equipment. It does not cross the public internet.

  • More predictable: No internet traffic shares the link, so delay and throughput vary less.
  • Higher capacity: Ports of 1, 10 or 100 Gbps; smaller shares are sold through partner companies.
  • Slower to set up: Often weeks, because a physical circuit must be ordered and connected.
  • Not encrypted by default: Private is not the same as secret. Encryption must be added if the data needs it.
Provider Service Names: AWS Direct Connect Azure ExpressRoute Google Cloud Interconnect

Comparison: Site-to-Site VPN vs. Dedicated Connection

Slide 12/42

💡 Hybrid Design Rule: A common design uses both: the dedicated link carries the traffic and a VPN waits as the backup.

Worked Calculation Exercise: Will It Fit?

Slide 13/42

Problem: A hospital copies 2 TB of new data to the cloud every night between 23:00 and 07:00 (8 hours). Assume 1 TB = 10^12 bytes and 70% effective throughput. Which is enough: a 100 Mbps VPN, a 1 Gbps VPN, or a 10 Gbps dedicated link?

time (s) = [ data (bytes) × 8 ] ÷ [ 0.7 × line rate (bits/s) ]
Calculation for 1 Gbps (10^9 bits/s):
time = (2 × 10^12 × 8) ÷ (0.7 × 10^9) ≈ 22,857 seconds (≈ 6.35 hours)
Minimum required line rate for 8 hours (28,800 s): about 794 Mbps
Line Time for 2 TB Fits in 8 hours?
100 Mbps about 63.5 hours No
1 Gbps about 6.35 hours Yes, with little room to grow
10 Gbps about 38 minutes Yes, easily

Additional factors to check: The provider's per-tunnel limit, data growth, time to retry a failed copy, and encryption for patient data.

PART 3

Connecting Cloud Networks & Provider Services

Slides 14/42 – 18/42

Peering

Slide 14/42

Organizations rarely have one cloud network. They separate test from production, and one application from another.

Peering joins two cloud networks so they reach each other on private addresses, over the provider's own network. No VPN, no internet.

  • Both networks' address blocks must not overlap.
  • Each side needs routes to the other (some providers add them automatically; AWS does not).
  • Firewall rules must allow the traffic; peering itself opens nothing.
  • Peering can often join networks in different regions or accounts.
Provider Names: VPC peering (AWS) VNet peering (Azure) VPC Network Peering (Google Cloud)

Peering is NOT Transitive

Slide 15/42

A is peered with B, and B with C. A still cannot reach C.

Peering carries traffic only between its own two networks, never through one of them to a third. Each pair that must talk needs its own peering, or a hub.

The Cost of That Rule (Full Mesh Formula):

Connecting every network to every other takes n(n - 1) / 2 peerings:

5 networks ➔ 10 peerings
10 networks ➔ 45 peerings
20 networks ➔ 190 peerings

Each with its own routes to keep correct.

Hub and Spoke (Transit Gateway)

Slide 16/42
Full mesh: 5 networks, 10 peerings
Hub: 5 networks, 5 attachments
  • A hub (transit gateway) is a central router that each network connects to once. Its routes decide which networks may talk.
  • The VPN or dedicated link to the office also attaches to the hub once.
  • Managed hubs are charged per attachment and per GB.
Provider Names: AWS Transit Gateway Azure Virtual WAN Google Cloud Network Connectivity Center

  • Conditional forwarding: The office resolver sends queries for internal.example.com, and only those, to the cloud's inbound resolver endpoint (10.20.2.53).
  • That traffic needs its own route and firewall rule: UDP and TCP port 53.
🔑 Key Rule: The VPN supplies a path, not names. Name resolution needs its own configuration.

PART 4

Routes and Redundancy

Slides 19/42 – 21/42

Static and Dynamic Routes (BGP)

Slide 19/42

Static route

Written by hand: "send 10.0.0.0/16 to the VPN".

  • Simple, easy to read
  • Does not react to a failure by itself
  • Must be edited by hand when a range is added

Dynamic route

Learned automatically: routers tell each other which ranges they can reach.

  • Withdraws a failed path and moves traffic to a backup after a short convergence time
  • New ranges appear without editing
BGP (Border Gateway Protocol)

The protocol used between the office and the cloud is BGP (Border Gateway Protocol), the same protocol that joins the networks of the whole internet. Dedicated links use it; VPNs can use it or static routes.

⚠️ Exam Limitation: BGP tells each side which ranges are reachable; it does not test whether the database answers.

Two Links Are Better Than One, If They Fail Separately

Slide 20/42
One office router
(Single Point of Failure)
───►
One internet line
(Single Point of Failure)
── tunnel 1 ──►
Cloud gateway A
── tunnel 2 ──►
Cloud gateway B
Availability Math (Suppose each link is available 99.9% of the time):
  • One link: down 0.001 × 8760 h ≈ 8.76 hours a year.
  • Two independent links: both down with probability 0.001² = 10^-6, about 32 seconds a year.
"Independent" is the hard part:

The two tunnels above share one router and one internet line, so one failure there stops both. Real redundancy needs different equipment, paths and providers, and the surviving link must carry the whole load.

Overlapping Ranges: The Problem That Returns

Slide 21/42
Situation: The office uses 10.0.0.0/16. A newly acquired company's cloud network also uses 10.0.0.0/16. Now the two must be joined.
  • Direct routing cannot work: 10.0.4.20 exists on both sides.
  • Proper fix: Renumber one network. Slow and disruptive, but permanent.
  • Workaround: NAT between them, so one side appears under a different range. It works, but DNS, logs and rules must all use the translated addresses.
  • Single-service alternative: If only one service is needed, a private endpoint or a proxy for that service can avoid joining the networks at all.
📋 Prevention Rule: Choose non-overlapping ranges on day one, and keep a written list of every range the organization uses (an IP address management plan).
PART 5

How to Troubleshoot a Network Problem

Slides 22/42 – 26/42

The 6-Step Troubleshooting Method

Slide 22/42
Step 1: Identify the problem: who, what, since when, what changed?
Step 2: Decide the probable cause: start with the obvious.
Step 3: Test that idea.
Step 4: Plan the repair, with a way to roll it back, and carry it out.
Step 5: Check that everything works, and prevent a repeat.
Step 6: Write down what you found and what you did.
What is different for networks: The problem is often between two machines, not on either one. So Step 3 means testing each part of the path in turn.

Step 1: State the Problem Exactly

Slide 23/42
❌ Weak report

"The cloud is down."

No source, no destination, no port, no time. Nothing to test.

✅ Useful report

From the office application 10.0.8.10, TCP connections to db.internal.example.com:5432 fail since 09:00. Cloud applications still connect. Last night's changes touched DNS and the database firewall.

Source Destination Protocol Port Since Scope Recent changes
10.0.8.10 db.internal.example.com TCP 5432 09:00 office only DNS, firewall

🎯 Test from the affected machine. A test from another machine may give a different answer.

Check in Order: From Name to Program

Slide 24/42
# Check Question Typical test
1 Name Does the name give the right address, as seen from the client? dig, nslookup
2 Route there Does the client side have a route to the server's range? Route tables
3 Route back Does the server side have a route back to the client's range? Cloud route table
4 Firewalls Do all filters on the path allow it, both ways? Rules, flow logs
5 Program Is the server listening on that address and port, and does it accept the request? nc, ss, the real client
  • This is the default order; a later step can be tested directly, for example by IP address when DNS fails.
  • Change one thing at a time and test again after each change.
  • Step 3 (Route back) is the one most often forgotten: the request arrives, the reply is lost, and it looks like a firewall problem.

Stateful and Stateless Filters

Slide 25/42
Stateful filter (AWS: security group)

Remembers each allowed connection and lets its replies back automatically. A rule for the request is enough.

Stateless filter (AWS: network ACL)

Checks every packet on its own, in each direction. The request and the reply each need a rule.

The reply in our example: The database replies to the client's ephemeral port, not to 5432. A stateless filter on the database subnet must allow outbound traffic to 10.0.8.0/24 on the ephemeral range, commonly 1024–65535.
⚠️ Exam Trap: A stateful filter is not open in both directions: a connection started from the other side still needs its own rule.

What Each Result Tells You (Connection Handshake & Error Clues)

Slide 26/42
Client Server
SYN: may I connect? ───────────►
◄─────────── SYN-ACK: yes
ACK: connected ───────────►
◄──────────► TLS negotiation (if used)
◄──────────► application answer (login, query)
No answer at all ➔ timeout
A reset instead ➔ connection refused
Name or trust check fails ➔ certificate error
Request rejected ➔ login or application error
  • Name not found: DNS problem, as seen from this client (check 1).
  • Timeout: A route or filter dropped the request or the reply, or the program never answered (checks 2–5).
  • Refused: Some device answered; often nothing listens on the port, or a filter rejects (check 5, then 4).
  • Login error: The network path works; check credentials.

Testing the Port and the Listener

Slide 28/42
On the office application host
$ nc -vz -w 3 10.20.4.15 5432
Connection to 10.20.4.15 5432 port [tcp/postgresql] succeeded!
-v verbose, -z test only, -w 3 wait at most 3 s. Success means a TCP connection was set up.
On the database host
$ ss -tln
LISTEN 0 128 127.0.0.1:5432 0.0.0.0:*
-t TCP, -l listening, -n numeric. This program listens only on 127.0.0.1, the loopback address: only programs on the same machine can reach it.
Critical Exam Rule (Loopback vs Remote Listener): These are two separate examples. A database listening on 0.0.0.0:5432 or 10.20.4.15:5432 accepts remote connections; one on 127.0.0.1:5432 never does, and no cloud firewall rule changes that.

Ping is Useful, and Often Misleading

Slide 29/42

Ping sends an ICMP "echo request" and waits for a reply.

Ping works
  • An ICMP path exists right now, both ways
  • Says nothing about port 443 or 5432
Ping fails
  • The machine may be down, OR
  • ICMP is simply not allowed; many cloud firewalls block it by default
Rule: Test the thing that actually matters: the real port, with nc, Test-NetConnection or curl. "Ping fails but the website works" is normal in the cloud.

Reading a Traceroute

Slide 30/42
$ traceroute 10.20.4.15
1   10.0.8.1      0.6 ms    office router
2   10.0.255.1    1.1 ms    office VPN device
3   * * *
4   * * *
  • Traceroute sends probes with a growing hop limit; each router that answers appears as one line, with its round-trip time. (Labels in the right-hand column are added for the slide; real output shows only addresses and times.)
  • * * * means no reply in time. It is NOT proof that traffic stops there: tunnels and cloud routers often do not answer.
  • Here the last responding hop is the office VPN device. Next: tunnel status, cloud routes, flow logs, and a test on the real port.

The Cloud's Own Tools

Slide 31/42

Inside the provider's network you cannot plug in a cable. The provider gives you other ways to see:

  • Flow logs: Traffic summaries with source, destination, port, bytes, and ACCEPT or REJECT. REJECT narrows the search to the filters; ACCEPT only says the network let it through. They record only from the moment they are switched on.
  • Path analysis tools: Read your routes and rules and report which setting blocks a path. A model of the configuration, not a live test.
  • Packet mirroring (where offered): Copies traffic to a machine running a packet analyzer.
  • VPN status and the provider status page: Is a tunnel down, is the problem on the provider's side?
Path Analysis Tool Names: AWS Reachability Analyzer Azure Network Watcher Google Cloud Connectivity Tests
PART 7

A Worked Troubleshooting Example

Slides 32/42 – 34/42

The Symptom and the First Tests

Slide 32/42
Symptom: Last night the database got a new firewall group and its name moved to a new private DNS zone. Since 09:00 the office application cannot reach db.internal.example.com on TCP 5432. Cloud machines still can.
office-app$ nslookup db.internal.example.com
** server can't find db.internal.example.com: NXDOMAIN
cloud-app$ nslookup db.internal.example.com
Address: 10.20.4.15
office-app$ nc -vz -w 3 10.20.4.15 5432   (by address, skipping DNS)
nc: connect to 10.20.4.15 port 5432 (tcp) timed out
  • The record exists, but the office cannot see it: a name problem on the office side (check 1).
  • Even by address the connection times out: something on the path drops it (checks 2–4). Cloud applications use the database, so it is running.
  • Conclusion: So there are at least two separate problems.

The Cloud-Side Evidence

Slide 33/42
Check Finding Conclusion
Tunnels and routes Both tunnels up; routes present on both sides Checks 2 and 3 pass
Flow logs at the database Office attempts on 5432: REJECT. Cloud attempts: ACCEPT A filter drops the office traffic
Database firewall group Allows 5432 only from the cloud application servers; 10.0.8.0/24 is missing Cause 1 (check 4)
Office DNS resolver No forwarding for the new zone; queries go to public DNS, which answers NXDOMAIN Cause 2 (check 1)
⚠️ Why Multi-Cause Testing Matters: Fixing only the firewall would still leave the application failing on the name, and someone would wrongly decide the firewall change was useless.

Repair and Check, One Change at a Time

Slide 34/42
  1. Firewall: Allow TCP 5432 from 10.0.8.0/24 only. Check: nc -vz 10.20.4.15 5432 from the office host now succeeds.
  2. DNS: Forward internal.example.com from the office resolver to the cloud's inbound endpoint. Check: nslookup from the office host returns 10.20.4.15.
  3. Application: Connect with the real client, by name, with the expected TLS settings and credentials, and run a read-only query.
  4. Side effects: Cloud applications still connect; other office subnets are still blocked.
  5. Document: Write down both causes and both changes; add "test from the office" to the change checklist.
🔒 Why not allow the whole 10.0.0.0/16? Least privilege: only the application subnet (10.0.8.0/24) needs the database.
PART 8

Slow, Not Broken

Slides 35/42 – 37/42

Four Reasons a Working Connection is Slow

Slide 35/42
Cause What it is How it shows
Round-trip latency (RTT) The time for one round trip; set mostly by distance Every small request is slow; pages making many requests in sequence are very slow
Bandwidth The line's nominal capacity (throughput is what you actually get) Large downloads are slow; the link shows near 100% use
Packet loss Some packets never arrive and must be sent again Slow and uneven transfers
Packet size (MTU) The largest packet the path accepts Small requests work, large transfers hang
Example: 20 requests in sequence at 80 ms round trip wait 20 × 80 ms = 1.6 s, whatever the bandwidth. Measure before paying for a faster line: a latency problem is not fixed by more bandwidth.

Why VPNs Have a Packet-Size (MTU) Problem

Slide 36/42
Original IP packet: 1500 bytes
VPN headers
Original IP packet: 1500 bytes (Exceeds 1500-byte path limit!)
▲ path limit: 1500 bytes
  • 1500 bytes is the common Ethernet MTU (maximum transmission unit). A VPN adds its own headers, so a full-size packet no longer fits inside the tunnel.
  • Path MTU discovery: The sender is told to use smaller packets by an ICMP message ("fragmentation needed" in IPv4, "packet too big" in IPv6). If a firewall blocks that message, large packets silently vanish.
The Typical Symptom: Login works and small pages load, but uploads and large query results hang.
3 Fixes (as the provider's VPN guide specifies): (1) Allow those ICMP messages, (2) Set the tunnel MTU correctly, or (3) Clamp the TCP maximum segment size (MSS).

Distance Limits One Connection's Speed

Slide 37/42

A sender may only have a limited amount of data "in flight" before the receiver confirms it. To keep a line full:

data in flight = bandwidth × round-trip time
Worked Example (1 Gbps to Northern Virginia, 84 ms round trip):
10^9 × 0.084 ÷ 8 = 10.5 MB must be in flight at once.
With only 1 MB in flight, one connection reaches about 95 Mbps, under a tenth of the line.
What to do: Measure first; modern systems tune TCP windows automatically. Then consider several connections in parallel, or place the data in a region closer to where it is used. This is why one file copy over a long distance often uses only a fraction of a 1 Gbps line.

Wrap-Up, Diagnostic Exercises, Master Glossary & Practical Task

Slides 38/42 – 42/42

Exercise: Find the Cause (All 5 Cases + Suggested Answers)

Slide 38/42
1. Networks A–B and B–C are peered. A cannot reach C.
Suggested Answer: Peering is not transitive; add A–C peering or a hub.
2. The office reaches cloud machines, but cloud machines cannot open a connection to the office printer server.
Suggested Answer: The office side does not accept connections started from 10.20.0.0/16; check the office firewall log, the route to the printer's subnet, and the printer server's own filter.
3. ping to the web server fails, but the website opens in a browser.
Suggested Answer: ICMP is blocked, which is common; test the real port instead.
4. Over the VPN, login and small pages work; uploading a report hangs.
Suggested Answer: Likely packet size (MTU) over the tunnel; confirm with a large ping that may not be fragmented.
5. curl to port 8080 answers "connection refused" at once.
Suggested Answer: Likely nothing listens on 8080, or a filter rejects; check on the server with ss -tlnp.

Site-to-site / remote-access VPN Joins two networks / joins one person's device.
Dedicated connection A private physical link to the provider; not encrypted by default.
Peering A private link between two cloud networks; not transitive.
Hub (transit gateway) A central router every network connects to once.
Conditional forwarding A DNS resolver sends queries for one domain to a chosen resolver.
Interface / gateway endpoint Private access to a provider service: an address in your subnet / a route-table entry (AWS).
Static / dynamic route, BGP Written by hand / learned from other routers; BGP exchanges the routes.
Ephemeral port The temporary client port that replies are sent to.
Stateful / stateless filter Replies allowed automatically / every packet checked on its own.
Timeout / refused No answer came back / some device answered with a reset.
Flow logs Summaries of accepted and rejected traffic; not every packet.
RTT / bandwidth / throughput Time for one round trip / nominal capacity / what you actually get.
MTU The largest packet a path accepts; smaller inside a VPN.

8-Point Lecture Summary

Slide 40/42
  1. A connection needs a name, a route there, a route back, firewall rules and a listening program, all at once.
  2. VPN: quick and cheap, over the internet, encrypted. Dedicated link: more predictable, high capacity, slow to set up, not encrypted by default.
  3. Every connection needs routes on both sides and non-overlapping ranges.
  4. Peering is not transitive; many networks call for a hub. Private names need DNS forwarding; provider services can use endpoints.
  5. Redundancy works only when the paths fail separately.
  6. Troubleshoot in order, from the affected machine, one change at a time.
  7. Timeout and refused are different clues; a failed ping proves little.
  8. Slow is not broken: tell latency, bandwidth, loss and packet size apart before paying for more.
