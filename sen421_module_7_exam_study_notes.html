PART 1

Starting Simple: Who Are You, and What May You Do?

Slides 3/37 – 5/37

Where We Are & Today's Core IAM Question

Slide 3/37

From the Security Topic:

  • Under shared responsibility, the customer configures who can access its data and settings in every service model. The provider secures the platform, and its own staff's access.
  • Least privilege: only the access needed, only for as long as needed.
  • In the Capital One case, a role with far too much permission turned one mistake into a major breach.
Today's Question: Hundreds of people and programs need some access to the cloud account. How do we know who each one is, give each exactly what it needs, and take it back when it is no longer needed?

A Comparison You Already Understand: Your Campus Card (The 4 Pillars of IAM)

Slide 4/37
🪪 On campus 🔐 In IAM terms ❓ Question
The card says who you are Identification Who do you claim to be?
Your photo or PIN shows you are the card's owner Authentication Can you prove it?
The system decides which doors open: the library yes, the server room no Authorization What may you do?
Every door records which card opened it, and when Accounting (auditing) What did you do?
🔑 Key Exam Rule: Authentication asks "who are you?"; authorization asks "what may you do?"

Why Identity is the New Perimeter

Slide 5/37
  • In an office, the network wall kept most people out.
  • In the cloud, the management console and APIs are on the internet. Stolen credentials — a password or an access key — work from anywhere, unless MFA, conditional access, or other controls stop them.
  • So the question "is this really our administrator?" now protects more than any firewall.
🚨 A Pattern from Real Incidents

Many cloud breaches involve no clever attack at all: attackers simply log in with stolen credentials, and then use whatever those credentials are allowed to do.

Identities: People, Groups, Roles, and Programs

Slides 6/37 – 8/37

Kinds of Identity

Slide 6/37
Identity What it is Example
User One person, with their own sign-in Ayşe, a database administrator
Group A collection of users used to grant permissions; not itself a sign-in "Database administrators"
Role Depends on the provider.
• AWS: an identity someone or something assumes for a while, getting temporary credentials.
• Azure and Google Cloud: a named set of permissions assigned to a user, group, or program on a scope. 		• AWS: "read the reports bucket", assumed by an app server.
• Azure: "Storage Blob Data Reader" on one storage account.
Workload identity An identity for a program, not a person The nightly backup job
✅ Good practice: grant through groups and roles rather than to individual users. When Ayşe changes team, you move her between groups instead of editing a dozen permissions.

Protecting the AWS Account Root User

Slide 7/37

When an AWS account is created, its email address becomes the root user. It can do everything, including closing the account.

RULE 1
Protect it with the strongest MFA available, ideally a hardware security key.
RULE 2
Create separate administrator identities for daily work.
RULE 3
Never create access keys for the root identity.
RULE 4
Lock the credentials away, with a documented recovery process, and use them only for the few tasks that require them.
RULE 5
Alert on every root sign-in.
🗝️ Analogy: Using root for everyday work is like carrying the master key of the whole university in your pocket to open your own office.

PART 3

Authentication: Proving Who You Are

Slides 9/37 – 14/37

Three Kinds of Proof & Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA)

Slide 9/37
🧠 Something you know

A password or PIN.

📱 Something you have

A phone, a hardware security key.

🫆 Something you are

A fingerprint, a face.

Multi-factor authentication (MFA) requires proof from two different kinds. A stolen password alone is then not enough.

⚠️ A Common Exam Trap

A password and a security question are NOT two factors: both are something you know. Two passwords are still one factor.

🔄 Plan Recovery Too

A lost phone must not lock an administrator out or let an attacker in through a weak reset.

Passwords: What the Current Rules Say (NIST SP 800-63B-4, July 2025)

Slide 10/37

The US standard NIST SP 800-63B-4, final in July 2025, changed long-held habits:

✅ Do

  • Require at least 15 characters when the password is the only factor (8 when used with MFA)
  • Should allow at least 64 characters, spaces included
  • Check new passwords against lists of leaked passwords
  • Allow password managers

❌ Do Not

  • Force mixtures of capitals, digits, and symbols
  • Force a change on a schedule — change when there is evidence of compromise (a leak, phishing, suspicious sign-ins)
  • Use password hints or security questions
Why? Forced complexity produces Password1!; forced rotation produces Password2!. Both are guessed early.

Length Beats Complexity (Offline Guessing Math)

Slide 11/37

An attacker who has stolen the stored password data guesses offline. Time to try every password of truly random characters (on average, half):

Password Possibilities Fast hash (10^11/s) Slow hash (10^5/s)
8 lowercase letters 26^8 ≈ 2.1 × 10^11 2 seconds 24 days
8 of any 94 characters 94^8 ≈ 6.1 × 10^15 17 hours about 1,900 years
12 lowercase letters 26^12 ≈ 9.5 × 10^16 11 days about 30,000 years
15 lowercase letters 26^15 ≈ 1.7 × 10^21 about 530 years about 530 million years
  • Twelve random lowercase letters outlast eight random "complex" characters; a predictable 12-letter phrase does not.
  • The storage matters as much as the password: here the slow hash multiplies the attacker's work a million times (an assumed ratio; it depends on the settings and the hardware).
  • Real people do not choose random passwords; attackers try leaked lists and common patterns first. Hence the check against leaked passwords.

Storing Passwords Correctly

Slide 12/37

Never store the password. Store a password-hash record from a vetted library: the salt, the algorithm, its cost settings, and the result.

User Password
+ Random Salt (unique per password)
───►
Slow Hash Function
Argon2id / bcrypt / scrypt
───►
Stored Record
salt + alg + cost + hash
  • Salt — a random value added to each password before hashing. Two users with the same password get different hashes, and precomputed tables become useless.
  • Slow hash — a function designed to be expensive: Argon2id where available, or bcrypt or scrypt. Each guess costs the attacker what a login costs the server; at 10^5 guesses a second, a billion guesses still take only about 2.8 hours — so length still matters.
  • A fast general hash such as plain SHA-256 is wrong for passwords, however strong it is for other uses.
🛠️ For your own projects: Do not write this yourself. Use your framework's password library, or let a managed identity service store passwords for you.

Not All MFA is Equal

Slide 13/37
Method How it works Weakness
SMS code A code sent by text message Phone number can be moved to the attacker's SIM; code can be phished
Authenticator app code A code, commonly 6 digits, that changes every 30 seconds Can still be typed into a fake login page
Push approval "Is this you?" on your phone Attackers send dozens of requests until a tired user taps yes
Security key or passkey The device proves itself only to the real website's address Must be kept safe and have a backup
🛡️ Phishing-Resistant MFA: Of these, only the last row (Security key or passkey) is phishing-resistant: a fake site at a lookalike address simply gets no answer. Any MFA is far better than none; the last row is the goal for administrators. Enrollment and recovery must be protected too.

The Providers Now Require MFA & Case Study: Snowflake Customers (2024)

Slide 14/37
AWS: MFA required for root users, from 2024.
Azure: Portal sign-ins from October 2024; CLI, PowerShell, IaC tools, and APIs (create, update, delete) from October 1, 2025, rolled out gradually.
Google Cloud: Phased enforcement during 2025.

Scope and dates differ by provider; check the current page before relying on them.

❄️ Case Study: Snowflake Customers, 2024
  • Snowflake is a cloud data platform.
  • Attackers used passwords stolen earlier by malware on employees' and contractors' computers — some from 2020 — and signed in to customer accounts that had no MFA.
  • About 165 organizations were notified as potentially exposed; data of several large companies was stolen and used for extortion.
  • The investigation found no evidence that a breach of Snowflake's own platform caused it. (Shared responsibility: customer credentials & missing MFA!)
PART 4

Authorization: Permissions and Policies

Slides 15/37 – 20/37

The 5 Parts of a Permission

Slide 15/37
Part Question Example
Effect Allow or deny? Allow
Principal Who? Ayşe, through her "report-writers" group
Action Doing what? Read files
Resource On what? Only the shop-reports bucket
Condition Under which circumstances? Only with MFA, only from the office network

A policy is a written set of such permissions. Providers write them in different ways; the principal or the condition may be implicit (a policy attached to an identity names no principal).

A Policy, Written Down (AWS Identity-Based Policy JSON)

Slide 16/37 
{
  "Version": "2012-10-17",
  "Statement": [
    {
      "Effect": "Allow",
      "Action": "s3:GetObject",
      "Resource": "arn:aws:s3:::shop-reports/*",
      "Condition": {
        "Bool": {"aws:MultiFactorAuthPresent": "true"}
      }
    }
  ]
}
Effect: allow or deny.
Action: read an object (not list the bucket).
Resource: any object in the shop-reports bucket.
Condition: only if the request carries MFA context.
Principal: the identity this policy is attached to.
💡 Important Notes: An AWS identity-based policy. Other policies can still allow or deny; objects encrypted with a KMS key also need KMS permission. Azure and Google Cloud express the idea as role assignments on a scope.

How the Decision is Made (Simplified, AWS-Style Policy Evaluation Logic)

Slide 17/37

When a request arrives, the cloud checks every policy that applies:

STEP 1
1. Start from "no"

Nothing is allowed unless a policy allows it — the implicit deny.

STEP 2
2. Look for an allow

If one policy allows the request, it may go ahead...

STEP 3
3. ... unless any policy explicitly denies it

An applicable explicit deny always wins over any allow.

Full Evaluation Layers: The full rule has more layers: organization guardrails, permissions boundaries, session policies, resource policies, cross-account rules. An allow must pass every limiting layer that applies.

Why this design: Adding permissions can only open what you meant to open. A deny in an organization guardrail — "never delete the audit logs" — cannot be undone by an allow inside the accounts it covers, even by their administrators.

Two Ways to Organize Permissions: RBAC vs. ABAC

Slide 18/37
Role-based (RBAC)

Permissions attach to job roles: "developer", "database administrator", "auditor". People are placed in roles.

  • Simple to understand and review
  • Roles multiply as exceptions pile up
Attribute-based (ABAC)

Permissions follow attributes of the user, the resource, and the context (time, network) — often tags: "a user in project=shop may manage resources tagged project=shop".

  • One rule covers hundreds of cases
  • Only as reliable as the tags
⚠️ Exam Warning on ABAC Tags: Most organizations combine them: roles for the broad shape, attributes for fine-grained limits. Either way, if nobody controls who can change the tags, ABAC can be bypassed by re-tagging.

Least Privilege in Practice

Slide 19/37

Nobody writes a perfect policy on the first day. A workable method:

  1. Start with the minimum for the task. (Broad "read only" roles can still expose sensitive data and settings.)
  2. Add permissions when a real task fails, one at a time.
  3. Use the provider's access analysis tools: they report which permissions were granted but not used in, say, 90 days.
  4. Review unused ones with their owner, then remove them — seasonal jobs and emergency tasks may need access only rarely.
🚨 The Shortcut That Causes Incidents

"Just give it administrator access, we will fix it later." Later rarely comes, and the program with administrator access becomes the most valuable thing an attacker can steal.

Worked Exercise: Allowed or Not? (Mehmet's Policies — Slide 20/37)

Slide 20/37
Policies that apply to the user Mehmet (assume nothing else applies):
  • P1: allow reading objects in shop-reports.
  • P2: allow reading and deleting objects in every bucket.
  • P3 (from the organization): deny deleting objects in audit-logs.
1. Read an object in shop-reports.
Answer: YES — P1 or P2 allows it.
2. Delete an object in shop-reports.
Answer: YES — P2 allows delete everywhere — probably more than intended.
3. Delete an object in audit-logs.
Answer: NO — the explicit deny in P3 wins over P2's allow.
4. Start a new virtual machine.
Answer: NO — nothing allows it: implicit deny.
🔍 Follow-up: P2 is the policy to question; least privilege says remove it.
PART 5

Identities for Machines and Programs

Slides 21/37 – 24/37

Programs Need Identities Too

Slide 21/37

An application server reads the reports bucket. How does it prove who it is?

❌ Wrong: A Stored Access Key

  • A long-lived key copied into a file on the machine
  • Works until someone deletes it — often never
  • Copied into images, backups, and Git

✅ Right: A Workload Identity Attached to the Machine

  • The machine gets temporary credentials from the provider; the SDK refreshes them
  • They expire automatically, usually within hours
  • No static key to copy or rotate by hand — though a compromised machine can still leak them, so keep them least-privilege
Provider Names: AWS IAM roles for EC2 (instance profiles) Azure managed identities Google service accounts attached to a VM

The Metadata Service: Convenient, and a Target (AWS EC2)

Slide 22/37

Each cloud machine can reach a special link-local address, the metadata service, that hands out its settings and its temporary role credentials.

🚨 Capital One Attack: SSRF

Server-side request forgery (SSRF) — the attacker made the WAF server send a request to the metadata service on her behalf, and received the role's temporary credentials.

🛡️ Defense: Require IMDSv2

IMDSv2 requires a session token obtained with a separate PUT request first. That blocks many SSRF paths, not a fully compromised server. Require IMDSv2 on every instance, and turn the metadata service off where nothing needs it.

Two lessons in one case: A role is safer than a stored key, but it is still a credential. What it can do must still follow least privilege.

Deploying Without Stored Keys: Workload Identity Federation

Slide 23/37

Automated build and deployment pipelines — for example GitHub Actions — need to change the cloud account. The old way stored a cloud access key in the pipeline's settings.

CI/CD Pipeline
(e.g. GitHub Actions)
1. Short-lived signed OIDC token ───►
Cloud Trust Rule
Checks issuer, audience, claims (repo + main branch)
───► 2. Returns
Temporary Credentials
For a specific role (No stored key!)
  • Workload identity federation: the pipeline proves its identity with a short-lived signed token from its own platform; the cloud checks it and returns temporary credentials for a specific role.
  • No long-lived cloud access key needs to be stored in the pipeline.
  • The trust rule must check the token's issuer, audience, and claims — for example one repository and the main branch. Branch limits help only if changes to workflows and branch protection are controlled too.
  • It uses the same standard (OpenID Connect) as signing in with a university or Google account (Part 6).

If You Must Use Access Keys

Slide 24/37

Sometimes a long-lived key cannot be avoided, for example for an old tool that supports nothing else:

  • One key per person or program; never share keys.
  • Least privilege on the identity behind the key.
  • Store it in a secrets manager, never in code.
  • Rotate it with an overlap: create the new key ➔ switch ➔ test ➔ then disable the old. Remove unused keys (for example after 90 days — an organizational choice).
  • Alert when a key is used from an unusual place.
🚨 If a key leaks: deactivate it first, then read the audit log for everything it did — and remove what it created (new users, keys, sessions).
PART 6

Federation and Single Sign-On

Slides 25/37 – 28/37

One Identity, Many Systems: IdP, Federation, and SSO

Slide 25/37
Identity provider (IdP)

The one directory where an organization keeps its people. Examples: Microsoft Entra ID, Google Workspace, Okta.

Federation

The cloud, and other applications, trust the IdP to say who a user is, instead of keeping their own list of users and passwords.

Single sign-on (SSO)

The user signs in once, with MFA, and can open each connected application without signing in again. Each application still decides what the user may do.

  • One place to enforce MFA and password rules.
  • One place to stop new sign-ins when someone leaves — sessions already open elsewhere last until they expire or are revoked.
  • Fewer passwords, so fewer reused ones.

How a Federated Sign-In Works (Simplified 6-Step Sequence Diagram)

Slide 26/37
User's browser
Cloud console or application
Identity provider directory + MFA
1. open the console ───────────────────────►
◄─────────────────────── 2. "sign in at the IdP first"
3. password + MFA (only the IdP sees them) ─────────────────────────────────────────────►
◄───────────────────────────────────────────── 4. signed token: "this is Ayşe, valid for a short time"
5. token ───────────────────────────────►
6. checks the token, then her permissions
🔐 What the Application Checks: The application never sees the password. It checks the signed token — signature, issuer, audience ("meant for me?"), expiry — and then applies its own authorization.

The Standards Behind It: SAML 2.0, OIDC, OAuth 2.0, SCIM

Slide 27/37
Standard Answers Typical use
SAML 2.0
(Security Assertion Markup Language)		 Who is this user? (authentication) Company sign-in to business applications and cloud consoles; older and XML-based, still widely used
OpenID Connect (OIDC) Who is this user? (authentication) "Sign in with Google / Microsoft"; web and mobile apps; pipelines (Part 5)
OAuth 2.0 What may this app do — for a user, or on its own? (authorization) "Allow this app to read your calendar"; service-to-service access
SCIM
(System for Cross-domain Identity Management)		 Keep user lists in step Creating, updating, and removing accounts and group memberships; it does not sign anyone in
💡 OpenID Connect is built on top of OAuth 2.0 — the same authentication versus authorization distinction from the start of today.

Joiners, Movers, Leavers & Privilege Creep

Slide 28/37
1. Joiner

A new employee receives the access of their role on the first day, not a copy of a colleague's access.

2. Mover

Someone changes team: the new access is added and the old access is removed.

3. Leaver

A verified sequence, timed to the departure: disable sign-in, revoke sessions and tokens, remove grants and access keys, remove local and application accounts, rotate shared secrets the person knew, and confirm it worked.

📈 Privilege creep

Movers usually get new access and keep the old. After five years in five teams, one person can do almost everything — and nobody decided that.

An IdP with SCIM automates much of this; check the systems it does not reach.

PART 7

Governance: Keeping Access Under Control

Slides 29/37 – 32/37

Privileged Access

Slide 29/37

Administrator access is what attackers want most. Treat it differently:

Just-in-time access

Nobody holds administrator rights permanently. A person requests them for a task, passes MFA and approval, and they expire after a few hours; every activation is logged.

Separation of duties

No single person can both make a payment change and approve it, or both write code and approve its release.

Break-glass account

An emergency administrator account for when normal sign-in is broken: strongly protected, not dependent on the normal IdP or MFA service, tested regularly, alerting on every use.

Example of a just-in-time tool: Microsoft Entra Privileged Identity Management.

Conditions and Context: Conditional Access

Slide 30/37

A correct password and MFA are not the only evidence. Access rules can also ask:

🌍 Where: From an expected country, or from the office or VPN?
💻 Which device: A managed, updated company laptop?
⚠️ How risky is this sign-in: Unusual for this person: new country, impossible travel, known-bad address?
Conditional access: allow, require extra proof, or block, depending on these answers.

These are risk signals, not proof — VPNs and shared exits confuse location — and users need a way to recover from a false block. This is zero trust from the security topic in practice.

Review, Record, Repeat

Slide 31/37
  • Access reviews — regularly (quarterly is a common choice), managers confirm who in their team still needs each access. Unconfirmed access is removed.
  • Accounting — the audit log records who did what. Each person needs their own identity: a shared "admin" login destroys attribution. (Programs sharing a role still leave session IDs.)
  • Alerts on the events that matter: root sign-in, new administrator, MFA removed, permission changes, break-glass use, a policy allowing *.
⚖️ Why auditors care: KVKK and GDPR ask organizations to show who can access personal data. Without reviews and logs, nobody can answer.

Worked Exercise: Name the IAM Problem and the Fix (All 5 Scenarios + Suggested Answers)

Slide 32/37
1. Five developers share one administrator login "to save time".
Suggested Answer: no accountability, and one leak exposes all — individual identities in a group, with MFA.
2. A staff member who left six months ago can still sign in to the cloud console.
Suggested Answer: a missing leaver process — disable the account, revoke sessions and keys, remove local accounts, verify; then automate with SSO and SCIM.
3. A backup script on a cloud machine uses an access key saved in a file.
Suggested Answer: a long-lived key — attach a role to the machine.
4. The root account is used every day, without MFA.
Suggested Answer: root misuse — strong MFA, separate admin identities, lock root away.
5. After three team changes, a tester can still delete production databases.
Suggested Answer: privilege creep — access reviews and removal on every move.

8-Point Lecture Summary

Slide 35/37
  1. Authentication is who you are; authorization is what you may do.
  2. Grant through groups and roles where possible; the root identity is locked away.
  3. Passwords: length, leak checks, slow salted hashes — not forced complexity or rotation.
  4. MFA everywhere; phishing-resistant keys for administrators.
  5. Policies start from no; an explicit deny beats every allow; every limiting layer must agree.
  6. Programs use roles and temporary credentials, not stored keys.
  7. Federation and SSO give one place to enforce rules and stop sign-ins; leavers also need sessions and keys revoked.
  8. Review access regularly: privilege creep is the default if you do not.
